When a machine pulls the trigger, who is on trial?

An investigation into AI-driven autonomous weapons and whether they can ever be reconciled with the laws of armed conflict.

A Group Research Project Theme · Artificial Intelligence Read time · 14 min

01 · THE QUESTION

A research question.

For the first time in human history, weapons can identify, track and kill a human being without a person making the final decision. The military calls them lethal autonomous weapons systems — LAWS. Critics call them killer robots. We wanted to know whether they can lawfully exist.

To what extent can lethal autonomous weapons systems comply with the principles of international humanitarian law — or do they fundamentally undermine the laws of armed conflict?

This site is our answer. It draws on case law, treaty texts, military doctrine, and the work of legal scholars, ethicists, and weapons engineers to reach a verdict.

02 · DEFINITION

What is an autonomous weapon, exactly?

There is no single agreed definition. The International Committee of the Red Cross calls them weapons that "select and apply force to targets without human intervention." The US Department of Defense uses a similar formulation. The disagreement begins where autonomy begins — and that is a spectrum, not a switch.

03 · THE LAW

Four rules a weapon must obey.

International humanitarian law — the body of rules governing armed conflict — rests on four principles. Every weapon, every commander, every soldier is bound by them. The question is whether a machine can be.

Distinction

Combatants must be distinguished from civilians. Force may only be directed at military targets.

Proportionality

Civilian harm must not be excessive relative to the anticipated military advantage.

Precaution

Constant care must be taken to spare civilians. Attacks must be cancelled if circumstances change.

Accountability

When civilians are unlawfully killed, a person must be capable of being held legally responsible.

04 · IN THE FIELD

This is not science fiction.

AI-enabled and partially autonomous weapons are already in use. Four cases anchor the debate.

LIBYA · 2020

Kargu-2

Turkish-made loitering drone

A UN Panel of Experts reported that a Kargu-2 drone "hunted down and remotely engaged" retreating fighters — possibly the first documented case of an autonomous weapon attacking humans without explicit human command.

UKRAINE · 2022–

Lancet & Switchblade

Russian and US loitering munitions

Both sides field "loitering munitions" — drones that circle a target zone and dive on a chosen target. AI-assisted targeting features have been reported on multiple platforms.

GAZA · 2023–

Lavender & Gospel

Israeli AI targeting systems

Investigative reporting in 2024 described Israeli military use of AI systems to identify and recommend targets at a scale and speed that critics argue erodes meaningful human judgement in life-and-death decisions.

GLOBAL · ONGOING

The CCW Talks

UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons

Since 2014 a UN Group of Governmental Experts has debated regulation of LAWS. As of 2026, no binding treaty exists. In 2023 the UN General Assembly called for one.

05 · THE DEBATE

Two arguments. One unresolved.

The literature divides cleanly. We have read on both sides; here is the strongest version of each case.

For permitting LAWS

  • Precision. Machines do not panic, exhaust, or hate. They may apply the rules of distinction and proportionality more consistently than soldiers under fire.
  • Lawful inevitability. Defensive autonomous systems (Iron Dome, Phalanx) already exist and save lives. A blanket ban is too late.
  • Strategic deterrence. If adversaries develop LAWS, refusal to do likewise creates a dangerous capability gap.
  • Regulate, do not ban. The right answer is robust testing, meaningful human control standards, and accountability rules — not prohibition.

For banning LAWS

  • The accountability gap. When a machine wrongly kills a civilian, no one is clearly responsible. This violates the basic premise of the laws of war.
  • Inability to judge context. Distinction and proportionality are moral judgements about human meaning. Algorithms recognise patterns, not intent.
  • Lower threshold for war. If wars can be fought without sending citizens to die, leaders may resort to force more readily.
  • Precedent. Blinding lasers and chemical weapons were pre-emptively banned. LAWS deserve the same treatment before proliferation.
06 · THE GAP

Who answers for a machine's mistake?

This is the hardest question in the field. A LAWS misidentifies a school bus as a troop transport and strikes it. Who is criminally liable?

Our respondents blamed the government or military first (mean rank 2.05) and the programmer last (mean rank 4.12). That ordering is revealing. Public intuition tracks the doctrine of command responsibility — the idea that whoever chose to deploy a weapon owns its consequences. Crootof (2015) builds her case for regulation on precisely this instinct, arguing for strict liability on the states and commanders who field autonomous systems.

But the instinct and the law come apart. Command responsibility requires that the commander knew, or should have known, what the weapon would do. The defining feature of a genuinely autonomous system is that its behaviour in a novel situation is not reliably foreseeable — which is the exact condition that breaks the doctrine. Every link in this chain can plausibly disclaim responsibility, and the machine cannot be tried at all. This is the accountability gap: for Human Rights Watch (2012) it is the single decisive argument for a pre-emptive ban.

What this tells us

The most striking number is not the highest one. 91% insist someone must remain accountable — near-unanimity. Yet as section 06 shows, the law currently cannot guarantee that anyone will be. Our respondents are describing a moral requirement that the legal system, as it stands, is unable to meet. That distance between what people demand and what the law can deliver is the accountability gap, measured.

The second striking number is the lowest. On distinction, opinion splits almost evenly — 56% think a machine would do worse than a human soldier. Compare that to the 87% who are uncomfortable with machine killing overall. Our respondents are far more confident in their moral objection than in the technical one. This matters for our research question: Sharkey (2012) rests the case against LAWS on the technical claim, while Schmitt and Thurnher (2013) argue legality is case-by-case precisely because that technical claim may not hold forever. Our data suggests the moral objection is the more durable of the two.

Read together, the survey supports a specific position rather than a blanket one: opposition here is driven less by doubt that machines can shoot accurately than by the conviction that no one should be able to walk away from the decision.

Limitations: n = 50, drawn from a single foundation-year cohort and self-selecting. The sample is not representative of any wider population, and respondents were given a short briefing on LAWS before answering, which may have framed their responses. The findings are indicative of informed student opinion, not generalisable public opinion.
